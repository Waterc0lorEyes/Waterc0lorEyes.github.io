Translation Reflection

In the process of translating and proofreading four different types of texts, I conducted an in-depth practice and reflection on translation techniques. This experience has given me a clearer understanding that high-quality translation is essentially a decision-making process based on deep comprehension. Its core lies in bridging the gap between languages and cultures to achieve accurate and fluent information delivery.

First, the accurate translation of terminology is the cornerstone of professional texts. However, this not only requires literal correspondence but also contextual adaptation. In corporate press releases, a subtle choice of preposition, such as using the neutral “not including” rather than the potentially exclusive “excluding” to describe ranking rules, reflects the pursuit of objectivity and rigor in business texts. In the translation of Chinese classical dance theory, terminology handling requires a more systematic approach. For example, ensuring that “式” in “掌式” and “拳式” is consistently translated as “form” maintains the internal coherence of the terminology system. For culture-specific items like “行当,” it is necessary to delve into their essence, which refers to the classification of opera roles, and translate them as “role types” to avoid misunderstandings caused by literal translation. These cases illustrate that the accuracy of terminology directly impacts the credibility of the translation in professional fields.

Second, the restructuring of sentence patterns is key to ensuring that the translation conforms to the norms of the target language. Chinese often uses run-on sentences connected by meaning, while English emphasizes hypotaxis and requires explicit logical connectors. This difference is particularly evident in translation. When handling corporate news, it is necessary to integrate multiple parallel verb phrases in Chinese into complex sentences in English with clear main and subordinate structures. For complex long sentences in social science academic works, splitting techniques are often required to convert embedded clauses, appositives, and other components into short sentences that align with the reading rhythm of Chinese, making logical relationships clear at a glance. Additionally, adjusting the focus of information is crucial. For example, placing the adverbial phrase “lasted for three days” at the beginning of a sentence aligns with the English convention of stating time and location upfront. These adjustments are all aimed at breaking free from the constraints of the source language structure and making the translation truly natural and fluent.

Finally, the explication of logic and cultural communication are important aspects of enhancing the readability of translations. Some logical relationships in Chinese are often implicit in the context and need to be made explicit during translation. For instance,for culture-loaded terms like “兰花指” and “空拳,” a strategy of literal translation with brief explanations is adopted to preserve cultural characteristics while ensuring comprehension. This process requires the translator to be not only a language practitioner but also a bridge for cultural communication.

Through reflecting on the translation of these four texts, I have come to understand that high-quality translation requires the translator to delve into the core of the text, accurately grasp its professional terminology, sentence logic, and cultural connotations, and reconstruct it according to the norms of the target language. Whether it is the emphasis on a rigorous tone in corporate news, the construction of a terminology system in theoretical articles, or the explication of complex logic in academic discourse, the core lies in making well-founded decisions to ensure that the translation remains faithful to the essence of the original text while conforming to the expressive habits of the target language.
