## Introduction

When exploring the diversity and complexity of translation, the short story "Cat in the Rain" serves as an ideal case study. The narrative delicately portrays the deepening emotional distance between a husband and wife due to communication barriers, as well as the individual's loneliness in real life and yearning for freedom. In a persistent drizzle, a lone cat becomes a symbol of the wife's inner conflicts and desires. By contrasting two different translation styles, this article delves into how the story's realistic style demands that translators find a balance between the subtle nuances of language emotion and the natural fluidity of dialogue. It examines how various translation strategies impact the reading experience, using specific examples from two distinct English-to-Chinese translations of the short story "Cat in the Rain." Furthermore, it explores translation challenges such as language style reproduction, cultural transplantation, and emotional transmission through systematic contrastive analysis of the translations.

The analysis focuses on how the translators handle Hemingway's signature "iceberg theory" writing style, where much of the meaning lies beneath the surface. The economy of language, the understated emotions, and the symbolic significance of mundane details all pose unique challenges for the translator. This study aims to provide practical insights into the translation of minimalist literary works and contribute to the broader discussion of literary translation strategies.

## Source Text: Cat in the Rain by Ernest Hemingway

There were only two Americans stopping at the hotel. They did not know any of the people they passed on the stairs on their way to and from their room. Their room was on the second floor facing the sea. It also faced the public garden and the war monument. There were big palms and green benches in the public garden. In the good weather there was always an artist with his easel. Artists liked the way the palms grew and the bright colors of the hotels facing the gardens and the sea. Italians came from a long way off to look up at the war monument. It was made of bronze and glistened in the rain. It was raining. The rain dripped from the palm trees. Water stood in pools on the gravel paths. The sea broke in a long line in the rain and slipped back down the beach to come up and break again in a long line in the rain. The motor cars were gone from the square by the war monument. Across the square in the doorway of the cafe a waiter stood looking out at the empty square.

The American wife stood at the window looking out. Outside right under their window a cat was crouched under one of the dripping green tables. The cat was trying to make herself so compact that she would not be dripped on.

"I'm going down and get that kitty," the American wife said.

"I'll do it," her husband offered from the bed.

"No, I'll get it. The poor kitty out trying to keep dry under a table."

The husband went on reading, lying propped up with the two pillows at the foot of the bed.

"Don't get wet," he said.

The wife went downstairs and the hotel owner stood up and bowed to her as she passed the office. His desk was at the far end of the office. He was an old man and very tall.

"Il piove," the wife said. She liked the hotel-keeper.

"Si, si, Signora, brutto tempo. It is very bad weather."

He stood behind his desk in the far end of the dim room. The wife liked him. She liked the deadly serious way he received any complaints. She liked his dignity. She liked the way he wanted to serve her. She liked the way he felt about being a hotel-keeper. She liked his old, heavy face and big hands.

Liking him she opened the door and looked out. It was raining harder. A man in a rubber cape was crossing the empty square to the cafe. The cat would be around to the right. Perhaps she could go along under the eaves. As she stood in the doorway an umbrella opened behind her. It was the maid who looked after their room.

"You must not get wet," she smiled, speaking Italian. Of course, the hotel-keeper had sent her.

With the maid holding the umbrella over her, she walked along the gravel path until she was under their window. The table was there, washed bright green in the rain, but the cat was gone. She was suddenly disappointed. The maid looked up at her.

"Ha perduto qualque cosa, Signora?"

"There was a cat," said the American girl.

"A cat?"

"Si, il gatto."

"A cat?" the maid laughed. "A cat in the rain?"

"Yes," she said, "under the table." Then, "Oh, I wanted it so much. I wanted a kitty."

When she talked English the maid's face tightened.

"Come, Signora," she said. "We must get back inside. You will be wet."

"I suppose so," said the American girl.

They went back along the gravel path and passed in the door. The maid stayed outside to close the umbrella. As the American girl passed the office, the padrone bowed from his desk. Something felt very small and tight inside the girl. The padrone made her feel very small and at the same time really important. She had a momentary feeling of being of supreme importance. She went on up the stairs. She opened the door of the room. George was on the bed, reading.

"Did you get the cat?" he asked, putting the book down.

"It was gone."

"Wonder where it went to," he said, resting his eyes from reading.

She sat down on the bed.

"I wanted it so much," she said. "I don't know why I wanted it so much. I wanted that poor kitty. It isn't any fun to be a poor kitty out in the rain."

George was reading again.

She went over and sat in front of the mirror of the dressing table looking at herself with the hand glass. She studied her profile, first one side and then the other. Then she studied the back of her head and her neck.

"Don't you think it would be a good idea if I let my hair grow out?" she asked, looking at her profile again.

George looked up and saw the back of her neck, clipped close like a boy's.

"I like it the way it is."

"I get so tired of it," she said. "I get so tired of looking like a boy."

George shifted his position in the bed. He hadn't looked away from her since she started to speak.

"You look pretty darn nice," he said.

She laid the mirror down on the dresser and went over to the window and looked out. It was getting dark.

"I want to pull my hair back tight and smooth and make a big knot at the back that I can feel," she said. "I want to have a kitty to sit on my lap and purr when I stroke her."

"Yeah?" George said from the bed.

"And I want to eat at a table with my own silver and I want candles. And I want it to be spring and I want to brush my hair out in front of a mirror and I want a kitty and I want some new clothes."

"Oh, shut up and get something to read," George said. He was reading again.

His wife was looking out of the window. It was quite dark now and still raining in the palm trees.

"Anyway, I want a cat," she said, "I want a cat. I want a cat now. If I can't have long hair or any fun, I can have a cat."

George was not listening. He was reading his book. His wife looked out of the window where the light had come on in the square.

Someone knocked at the door.

"Avanti," George said. He looked up from his book.

In the doorway stood the maid. She held a big tortoise-shell cat pressed tight against her and swung down against her body.

"Excuse me," she said, "the padrone asked me to bring this for the Signora."

## Translation Comparison and Analysis

### 1. Lexical Choices and Emotional Connotations

The translation of key emotional terms in "Cat in the Rain" significantly impacts the reader's interpretation. Hemingway's deliberate simplicity in word choice—using plain, everyday vocabulary—must be preserved while ensuring the emotional subtext remains accessible to Chinese readers.

For instance, the wife's repeated exclamation "I wanted it so much" carries layers of unfulfilled desire that go beyond the literal meaning. One translation renders this as "我太想要它了," which captures the surface meaning but loses some of the desperation. Another version translates it as "我多么想要它啊," which better conveys the wistful longing through the exclamatory particle "啊." The choice between these two approaches reflects a fundamental tension in literary translation: fidelity to the author's minimalist style versus the need to make implicit emotions explicit for a different cultural readership.

Similarly, the husband's dismissive "Oh, shut up and get something to read" presents a challenge. A literal translation such as "哦，闭嘴，找点东西读" preserves the bluntness but may sound overly harsh in Chinese. A softened version like "好了，别说了，去看点书吧" makes the dialogue more natural in Chinese but risks diminishing the emotional cruelty of the original. The translator must decide whether to prioritize the raw emotional impact or the naturalness of the target language dialogue.

### 2. Syntactic Restructuring and Narrative Rhythm

Hemingway's characteristic short, paratactic sentences create a staccato rhythm that mirrors the emotional emptiness of the characters' relationship. In Chinese translation, maintaining this rhythm while ensuring grammatical coherence requires careful syntactic restructuring.

The opening paragraph, with its series of simple declarative sentences ("There were only two Americans stopping at the hotel. They did not know any of the people..."), establishes the isolating atmosphere. Chinese translators face the challenge of whether to preserve this paratactic structure or to combine sentences for smoother reading. One translation maintains the short sentences, creating a similarly stark effect. Another combines some sentences, producing a more flowing but potentially less austere narrative.

The dialogue exchanges between the couple are particularly revealing. George's terse responses—"I'll do it," "Don't get wet," "I like it the way it is," "Yeah?"—contrast sharply with the wife's more expansive expressions of desire. This asymmetry in dialogue length and complexity is a crucial element of characterization that must survive translation.

### 3. Cultural Transplantation and Contextual Adaptation

The story is set in an Italian hotel with American protagonists, creating a tri-cultural context that poses unique translation challenges. The Italian phrases—"Il piove," "Si, si, Signora, brutto tempo," "Ha perduto qualque cosa, Signora?"—serve to establish the foreign setting and the wife's sense of displacement. Most Chinese translations preserve these Italian phrases in the original with Chinese annotations, maintaining the cultural texture while ensuring comprehension.

The cultural significance of certain details also requires careful handling. The "war monument" that "glistened in the rain" carries historical weight that may not immediately resonate with Chinese readers. The "big tortoise-shell cat" at the end—a symbol of substitution and compromised desire—requires the translator to find equivalent cultural resonance in Chinese.

### 4. The Iceberg Theory and Implicit Meaning

Hemingway's famous "iceberg theory" posits that the deeper meaning of a story should not be evident on the surface. In "Cat in the Rain," the emotional estrangement between the couple is never explicitly stated but is conveyed through their minimal dialogue and the oppressive atmosphere of the rain. The cat itself functions as a symbol—of the wife's desire for something to nurture, of her own feeling of being trapped and exposed, and ultimately of the impossibility of genuine fulfillment within her marriage.

For translators, this presents a fundamental dilemma: how much of the submerged meaning should be made explicit for readers unfamiliar with Hemingway's technique? Over-explanation risks violating the story's aesthetic principle, while under-explanation may leave Chinese readers puzzled about the story's significance. The most successful translations find a middle ground—using subtle lexical and syntactic choices to hint at deeper meanings without stating them outright, thereby preserving the participatory role of the reader in constructing meaning.

### 5. Specific Translation Challenges

**Repetition as a Stylistic Device:** Hemingway frequently repeats key words and phrases for rhythmic and thematic effect—"rain," "cat," "want," "like." Chinese, which typically avoids repetition in favor of variation, presents a challenge. Translators must decide whether to preserve the repetition (maintaining Hemingway's style but potentially sounding unnatural in Chinese) or to vary the language (smoother Chinese but losing the stylistic signature).

**Pronouns and Gender:** English pronouns like "she" and "her" referring to the cat subtly reinforce the identification between the wife and the cat. Chinese, with its single pronoun "它" for animals, loses this gendered connection. Creative solutions, such as occasionally using "她" for the cat, can help preserve the symbolic link.

**Register and Dialogue Naturalness:** The contrast between the wife's emotional speech and George's dismissive responses must be maintained. The hotel-keeper's formal politeness and the maid's cheerful demeanor add further layers to the register spectrum that translators must navigate.

## Conclusion

The comparative analysis of two Chinese translations of Hemingway's "Cat in the Rain" reveals that literary translation, particularly of minimalist texts, requires a delicate equilibrium between multiple competing demands: fidelity to the source text's stylistic features, naturalness in the target language, preservation of implicit meaning, and cultural accessibility. The most effective translations acknowledge these tensions and make principled choices rather than seeking a single optimal solution.

For short story translation specifically, the condensed form magnifies the importance of each lexical and syntactic choice. Every word carries proportionally greater weight, and subtle shifts in tone or connotation can significantly alter the reading experience. Successful translators of short fiction must therefore possess not only bilingual competence but also acute literary sensitivity and a deep understanding of the target readers' cultural and aesthetic expectations.
